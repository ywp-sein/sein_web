How to Cultivate a Good Habit?

27.08.2026 By Yuan-Wei Pi
HumanityLearning

On one side is the person we have already become. On the other side is the person we want to become.

How do we cultivate a good habit?

The principle seems surprisingly simple: our determination toward the new habit has to be stronger than the forces pulling us back to our old state.

Simple—but not necessarily easy.

Whenever we try to cultivate a new habit, there is a kind of tug-of-war happening inside us.

On one side is the person we have already become. Our body, thoughts, schedule, preferences, and routines have adapted to a certain way of living.

On the other side is the person we want to become.

So perhaps the first question is not:

How can I force myself to do this every day?

Perhaps it is:

Why do I want this new habit strongly enough to disturb the life I already have?

What are you actually fighting against?

Suppose I decide that I want to read every morning before going to work.

Why?

Maybe I want some personal reading time before entering my daily routine. I want new ideas and new input. I want to begin the day with a refreshed mind rather than immediately entering the flow of work.

That sounds good.

Then morning arrives.

I am sleepy.

Suddenly, the question is no longer theoretical. There are now two desires competing with each other:

I want to read.

I want to sleep longer.

This is where the habit is really being cultivated.

Which desire is stronger?

And more importantly:

Why should reading win?

If I cannot answer that question convincingly to myself, why would I expect my sleepy morning self to get out of bed?

Maybe reading gives me inspiration. Maybe it develops my discipline. Maybe it gives me ideas that eventually enter my work. Maybe it allows me to learn something outside the boundaries of my ordinary routine.

The more clearly I understand these reasons, the more strength the new habit has.

And I can ask the opposite question too:

What happens if I don't develop this habit?

I might gain another thirty minutes of sleep. But I lose that quiet period of reading. I lose an opportunity for new input. I lose the experience of beginning my day deliberately.

Now the tug-of-war looks different.

Can determination be cultivated?

This leads me to something I find more interesting than the habit itself.

Can determination also become a habit?

Usually, when we talk about cultivating habits, we focus on the outcome:

Read every morning.

Exercise regularly.

Learn something every day.

Go to bed earlier.

But underneath all these individual habits, perhaps there is another ability being developed:

the ability to cultivate a habit.

Every time I successfully change a habit, I practice recognizing what I want, understanding why I want it, noticing what resists the change, and repeatedly choosing the direction I believe is better.

In that sense, cultivating a good habit is itself a good habit.

Maybe that is much more valuable than any particular routine.

Because the world changes.

Our circumstances change.

Our knowledge changes.

And sometimes even a habit that was once good for us needs to be replaced.

What if cultivating a new good habit sometimes means overcoming an old good habit?

This is where things become slightly paradoxical.

What if the thing resisting our new habit isn't actually a bad habit, but an old good habit?

For example, perhaps I previously believed that optimizing my sleep was important. If I could sleep a little longer before work, that seemed like a good thing.

And perhaps it was.

I might have deliberately protected that extra sleep because I believed it was good for my health, my energy, and my day.

But now I want to introduce morning reading.

Suddenly, my old good habit—sleeping a little longer—becomes the counterforce against my new good habit.

Does that mean sleeping longer has become bad?

Not necessarily.

It may simply mean that I have discovered something else that I now value more. Perhaps instead of taking that extra sleep in the morning, I can go to bed earlier and create room for reading without sacrificing the rest I need.

This reveals something important:

A good habit does not have to become a bad habit before we decide to change it.

Sometimes our lives change. Our priorities change. Our knowledge changes. What served us well before may no longer be the best arrangement for where we want to go next.

So when an old good habit and a new good habit compete, what should we do?

Again, we return to the reason.

Why do I want the new habit?

What will it give me that the old habit cannot?

What am I willing to reorganize in order to make room for it?

This seems to be a critical moment in changing ourselves.

When we encounter resistance, can we identify exactly what is resisting us?

Sometimes it is laziness.

Sometimes it is immediate pleasure.

But sometimes it is an old belief or an old good habit that made perfect sense in an earlier version of our lives.

Then cultivating a new habit requires more than discipline. It also requires discernment.

Is the new habit actually better for me?

Why?

What has changed?

What am I willing to give up—or rearrange—for it?

What else has to change?

A new habit almost never exists by itself.

If I want to wake up earlier to read, perhaps I need to go to bed earlier.

If I go to bed earlier, perhaps something in my evening needs to change.

Then another part of my schedule moves.

One seemingly small habit reveals an interconnected system.

So cultivating a habit may not mean simply adding one more activity to our lives. It may mean reorganizing part of our lives around something we have decided matters more.

And then, eventually, we find a new rhythm.

We stabilize.

Until we learn something new.

Then perhaps we change again.

Isn't that strange?

We cultivate habits to create stability, yet we also need the ability to break that stability when a better way of living becomes possible.

Maybe the real goal, then, isn't to become static.

Maybe it is to become good at consciously moving from one stable state to a better one.

What do you need to “fast” from?

Fasting gave me an interesting way of thinking about this.

When fasting from certain foods, I may still like those foods. My preference hasn't magically disappeared. But I have made a decision not to eat them for a period of time.

At first, saying no can be difficult—especially when I am hungry.

But something interesting happens through practice.

The food can still be attractive without automatically controlling my response to it.

There is now a space between:

“I want this.”

and

“Therefore, I will have this.”

That space is discipline.

Perhaps cultivating a new habit also requires a kind of “fasting” from an old one.

The analogy isn't perfect. A fast is usually temporary, while replacing a habit may be a long-term change.

But the underlying question is similar:

Can I experience an old desire without automatically obeying it?

Can I want to sleep longer and still get up?

Can I want the old routine and still choose the new one?

Can I say:

Yes, I know what I want right now—but I also know what I want more in the long term.

So what is your reason?

Perhaps “How do I cultivate a good habit?” is not ultimately a question about techniques.

It is a question about motivation.

Why do I want this?

Why is this habit good?

What will it give me?

What will happen if I don't cultivate it?

What old habit, desire, or belief will resist it?

When that resistance appears, what reason will be strong enough to keep me moving?

And when my motivation becomes weaker, how can I deepen it rather than simply blaming myself for lacking discipline?

Because maybe discipline doesn't begin with forcing ourselves.

Maybe it begins with becoming so clear about why we want something that we are willing to repeatedly choose it over the alternatives.

And perhaps that is the habit beneath all good habits:

learning how to discover, strengthen, and hold on to a reason that is powerful enough to change the way we live.

So if there is a good habit you have been trying to cultivate, perhaps the question to ask yourself today is not:

“How can I make myself do it?”

Ask instead:

“Why do I want this habit—and is my answer strong enough to overcome the person I already am?”
