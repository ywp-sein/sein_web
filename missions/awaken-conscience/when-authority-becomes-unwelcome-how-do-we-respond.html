When Authority Becomes Unwelcome - How Do We Respond?

18.08.2026 By Yuan-Wei Pi
SocietyJusticeHumanity

What should society become, and what responsibility are we willing to accept to build it?

When a community encounters an authority that is not welcome, there are two fundamentally different ways it can respond.

Two Fundamentally Different Ways to Respond

The first is to recognize the problem while it is still at an early stage. People identify what is actually wrong, discuss the issues openly, question decisions when necessary, look for possible solutions, and accept some responsibility for improving the situation themselves. They do not simply ask, “Who is responsible for this?” They also ask, “What can we do about it?” In this process, they become part of the solution.

The second approach is much more passive. People notice the problems, but instead of addressing them directly, they remain silent. They complain privately. They wait to see whether someone else will speak first. They wait until they discover that other people share the same dissatisfaction. And they continue waiting until the dissatisfaction has accumulated enough that speaking up no longer feels like an individual risk.

Only then do they act.

By that point, the issue is no longer necessarily about solving the original problem. It can become about gathering enough collective dissatisfaction to justify removing the authority. Instead of asking what can be changed, people begin looking for someone with greater authority who can change it for them.

The difference between these two approaches is significant.

The first group responds to a problem when it is still manageable. They take initiative before frustration becomes collective resentment. They are willing to have difficult conversations, to propose solutions, and to accept that improving the situation also requires effort from themselves.

The second group waits for the problem to become large enough to force someone else to act. Their passivity allows dissatisfaction to accumulate. And because they waited until others spoke first, they can eventually participate without having taken the initial risk themselves.

When Fear Becomes Passivity

There is a certain logic behind this behavior. The first person to challenge an authority is often the most exposed. They may become the focus of attention, they may be criticized, or they may even become a target. So others remain silent until someone else creates a safe opening.

But this is also where fear can become an excuse for avoiding responsibility.

If people already recognize that something is wrong, waiting for someone else to say it first is still a choice. It means choosing safety over participation. And when everyone makes that choice, problems are allowed to grow until collective frustration reaches a threshold.

At that point, the group may suddenly appear very active. There may be many voices, many complaints, and a strong demand for change. But the activity came only after a long period of passivity.

This creates a recurring cycle: an authority becomes unpopular, people remain passive while dissatisfaction accumulates, someone eventually speaks, others join once it is safe, and collective pressure is used to remove the authority. Then a new authority takes their place. If the community has not changed its own behavior, eventually the new authority will become unacceptable too, and the same cycle begins again.

The authority changes. The pattern does not.

Principles or Position?

You can even see a broader version of this distinction in history. Revolutionary movements can be driven primarily by a belief that society should be fundamentally different, or they can become increasingly focused on who will control the new order and who will benefit from it. The American and French Revolutions, despite both being influenced by Enlightenment ideas and political grievances, developed very different political dynamics. This does not mean that one was purely idealistic and the other purely self-interested, but it does illustrate an important distinction between fighting for principles and fighting for position.

The first asks:

What should society become, and what responsibility are we willing to accept to build it?

The second asks:

Who should have the power once the current authority is gone, and what do we gain from the change?

That distinction also exists at the level of a community.

People who genuinely want to improve a situation tend to identify problems early, speak up, discuss solutions, and accept responsibility for what happens next. They are not simply waiting for someone else to remove an obstacle. They are willing to become part of the process of building something better.

People who are primarily motivated by their own benefits can remain passive until participation becomes safe or advantageous. They wait for someone else to take the first risk, and then join once there is enough collective support to protect them from the consequences.

Changing the Authority Is Not Solving the Problem

This is why solving a problem early is so different from waiting for collective dissatisfaction to become powerful enough to remove someone. In the first case, people are trying to improve the system. In the second, they may only be trying to change who is inside the system.

And there is something particularly uncomfortable when, after the authority has finally been removed, those who remained silent come forward to celebrate the result. The person who first identified the problem and took the risk of speaking may have stood alone when it mattered most. Others waited until there were enough voices to make participation safe.

There is a difference between supporting someone when they take the risk and joining them after the risk has already been taken.

The Responsibility to Be Part of the Solution

A healthy community should not need collective suffering or collective self-pity to discover the courage to address a problem. It should be capable of recognizing problems early, discussing them openly, and taking responsibility for solving them before they become crises.

Otherwise, people may spend their energy repeatedly replacing authorities instead of learning how to deal with authority, disagreement, and problems in a more responsible way.

Do you want to be part of the solution, or do you want to wait until the problem becomes so large that someone else has to fix it for you?
